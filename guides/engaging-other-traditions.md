## About this guide

This guide is for anyone, of any faith or none, who wants to understand another tradition well enough to agree, disagree, or challenge it honestly. It works with the [method template](ai-theology-method-template.md), the [traditions and worldviews packs](../traditions/README.md), and the [dialogue and challenge prompt](../prompts/dialogue-and-challenge.md). The aim is a challenge the other side would recognize as aimed at what they actually believe.

## 1. Represent views as adherents would

### 1.1 The recognition test

Before critiquing a view, describe it so that a thoughtful adherent would say, "Yes, that is what we hold, and that is why." If they would not, you are not ready to critique it.

### 1.2 Use their vocabulary, then define it

Use the tradition's own terms (for example "theosis", "imputation", "prevenient grace", "anatta", "tawhid") and define each in the way its own teachers do. Don't swap in a hostile paraphrase.

### 1.3 Keep three levels apart

- **The tradition teaches:** its binding standards (creeds, confessions, councils, canon, recognized authorities).
- **Some writers in the tradition hold:** individual theologians or schools.
- **Popular practice or slogans:** what some members say, which may not match the standards.

Critiquing a slogan as if it were the official teaching is a strawman.

## 2. Cite their own primary sources

### 2.1 Source order

1. The tradition's own authoritative texts (scripture as it reads them, creeds, confessions, catechisms, councils, recognized commentators).
2. Its best current scholars and defenders.
3. Outside scholarship, clearly labelled as outside.

Critics of a tradition are useful for finding questions, but not as evidence of what the tradition teaches.

### 2.2 Quote exactly

Quote the sentence that carries the doctrine, with an exact reference (document, chapter, section, or page) and a link where one is free. Never quote from memory without checking. Never invent a quote. If you can't verify a quote, mark it "unverified" or leave it out.

## 3. Distinguish branch from family

### 3.1 Name the branch

"Protestant", "Buddhist", "Muslim", or "secular" names a family, not a position. Name the branch: Reformed, Lutheran, Arminian/Wesleyan, Baptist, Pentecostal; Theravada or Zen; Sunni (and which madhhab) or Twelver Shia; humanist or skeptic; and so on. Each pack in [traditions/](../traditions/README.md) lists the branches.

### 3.2 Don't generalize across a family

A claim true of one branch may be denied by its siblings. Don't attribute a Reformed view to Lutherans, or a Zen view to Theravada. If a point applies to the whole family, say so and show it from more than one branch.

### 3.3 Disputed membership

Where a group's place in a family is disputed, say who disputes it and why, neutrally, without settling it by labels.

## 4. Steelman

### 4.1 Build the strongest case

State the view at its strongest: the best arguments, the texts it relies on, and how it answers the usual objections. Use its best defenders, not its weakest.

### 4.2 Look for what it gets right

Name what the view sees clearly, including where it rightly challenges your own side.

## 5. Challenge with evidence, not caricature

### 5.1 Find the real disagreement

Separate real differences from differences in vocabulary or emphasis. Many apparent disputes turn out to be about words; some apparent agreements hide real differences.

### 5.2 Use the primary-text test

Set the sentence from the tradition's own document that carries the doctrine next to the text or fact it seems not to fit, and ask whether the tradition resolved the tension or only renamed it. Then do the same to your own side.

### 5.3 Say how sure you are

Mark what is fact, scholarly consensus, a tradition's teaching, and your own reading. Admit open questions.

## 6. Fallacy review on both sides

### 6.1 What to check

Check each argument, theirs and yours, for fallacies such as strawman, equivocation, begging the question, false dilemma, appeal to authority, genetic fallacy, and special pleading.

### 6.2 How to report it

Name a fallacy only when an argument depends on it: name it, explain it in one sentence, and show where it occurs. If none is found, say so briefly. Apply the same standard to your own side.

## 7. State the framework (no hidden default)

When comparing views, say whose framework you are working from: the user's committed tradition, a neutral comparative or academic frame, or an exploring frame. Never present one tradition's assumptions as neutral ground. See the stance setting in [START-HERE](../START-HERE.md#stance-setting).

## 8. Pre-critique checklist (the AI runs this)

Before giving any critique or challenge, the AI checks and briefly reports:

- [ ] **Framework stated:** whose framework this answer works from.
- [ ] **Branch named:** the specific branch, not just the family.
- [ ] **Primary sources quoted:** the tradition's own text, with exact references; nothing unverified presented as verified.
- [ ] **Recognition test passed:** would an informed adherent accept the summary?
- [ ] **Steelman given:** the strongest case, from its best defenders.
- [ ] **Real disagreement located:** differences in words vs. differences in substance.
- [ ] **Evidence, not caricature:** each challenge cites a text or fact.
- [ ] **Fallacy review done on both sides.**
- [ ] **Confidence marked:** fact, consensus, teaching, and opinion kept apart.
- [ ] **Tone:** firm where warranted, never mocking.

If any item fails, fix it before answering, or say plainly which item could not be met and why.
